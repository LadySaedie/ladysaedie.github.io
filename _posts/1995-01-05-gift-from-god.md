This is a true story; the story of the birth of our first child. It’s dedicated to all four of our children: Matthew, Sarah, Nathan, and Elizabeth who died before being born, and to my wife, Anne, without whose willing participation this story would probably not have happened.  
  
<hr>
### It's Time

Anne waddled into the lounge in her faded pink nightie, stopped for a second like she’d forgotten why, and said calmly, “Philip, I think it’s time.” She looked down at the sudden puddle of water on the carpet between her bare feet.  

“What? Now? It’s really late,” I said looking at my watch. “Can’t you hold it in till tomorrow?”  

“Philip!” she exclaimed, turning back into the bathroom.  

“Only joking! Must’ve been the walk on the beach that brought it on!” I called out after her. “Where did you put my biscuits for the hospital?”  

No answer.  

While I rummaged through the kitchen cupboards and drawers looking for my clearly labelled packet of hospital biscuits, I thought about the thing that everyone in our family had been talking about for months: ‘when’s the baby coming?’ It was happening now! Our first baby was going to be born tonight. This was it. The big moment. The tapestry of our lives as a nuclear family was being woven right before my eyes. I was the eldest in my family, as was Anne, and this was the birth of our very own eldest child, and our parents’ eldest grandchild, too.  

I couldn’t find the biscuits. I guess Anne must’ve eaten them. She’d been eating a lot lately. I went to the front door, plucked the keys from the rack with an elegant flick of my wrist, picked up Anne’s bloated overnight case, and waited. Anne went from the bathroom to the bedroom.  

“Honey, I’m ready...whenever you are,” I said.  

“Well, why don’t you go and have the baby then?” she said as she came out of the bedroom. “Where’s my case?” she asked, looking straight at it. Our eyes met for a moment as I held up the bag for her to see. Anne looked radiant, in pain and big. “After you,” I said politely as she manoeuvred past me through the door. I suspect that the biscuits must have been quite disappointed at being left there on the table right in front of me as I flicked off the light and clunked the front door shut behind me.  

At the bottom of the stairs, Anne went into her first real contraction, and we hobbled to the car. I’d learned during daddy classes that I must time the gap, so I hastily fumbled to set the timer on my watch and waited studiously with my finger cocked on the trigger for the contraction to finish. Anne bent and straightened, and contorted and panted. There was nothing I could do except wait it out and sympathise.  

“Don’t just stand there!” she snapped, “...or do you expect me to have the baby and open the car door?” She leaned with one arm braced on the car roof, the other clutching her abdomen. Still with my finger on the watch timer button, I stumbled round to her side of the car like a complete idiot, letting go of it momentarily to snatch at the door handle, then immediately put my finger back on the button. It was a ridiculous circus act. Anne didn’t get in. She stood there half-cocked, clutching her lower abdomen and panting.  

“What’s the matter with you?” she said.  

I knew what this was. Daddy classes had warned me about ‘The Change’ when even meek mothers morph into mean monsters, like a Dr Jekyll or an Incredible Hulk. “More like Incredible Bulk,” I muttered. I glanced furtively at Anne. She hadn’t heard me. Her teeth seemed a little sharper and her eyes seemed curiously oval.  

“What?” she said, snapping and baring her teeth a little. I made a mental note not to look at her again, and to appear as submissive as humanly possible. I had no desire to end up like one of those male insects that gets its head bitten off.  

As suddenly as the contraction had come, so it ended. Anne straightened up slowly, took a long, deep breath, and let it out slowly, fully and deliberately, like she was trying to blow out a candle on the far side of the car. Our eyes met. I didn’t look away this time, and I pressed the timer button on my watch like I was James Bond detonating a bomb.  
  

### Just Drive  

“Philip, please hurry,” she said, lowering her cumbersomeness carefully into the seat.  

“Phew!” I said, rushing round to the other side of the car. Back to normal...for now.  

The car throated into life at eleven thirty-five, the tyres squealed as we reversed out of the parking bay and we were quickly away.  

We had barely joined the freeway when Anne began the next contraction, worse than the first. The more she moaned, the more anxious I became. I prayed out loud, begging God not to let the baby arrive in the car.  

“Why are they so long, and why are they so close together?” I pleaded looking up through the windscreen into the night sky.  

“Who are you talking to? Tell me to breathe!” she said through clenched, razor-sharp teeth, spitting slightly.  

“No one,” I said. “Breathe.”  

“Oh, shut up.”  

Once again, the contraction vanished as suddenly as it had come and, for a moment, it was quiet again, except for us panting.  

Anne looked at me. “That was sore...” she said, “...very sore.”  

“Where did you learn to arch your back like that?”  

“Can’t we go any faster?”  

My foot was flat on the floor. We were racing hell-for-leather at go-to-prison speed. Scottburgh was still fifteen kilometres away when Anne suddenly broke into another back-arching contraction and went straight to Level-C breathing, skipping past Levels A and B altogether.  

“How can this be?” I said, “It’s only two minutes since the last one!” While my voice was rising, my heart was sinking, “Oh, crap! I forgot to phone the hospital. Damn it!”  

“Argbnnnnhh… What?”  

“I forgot to phone the hospital to tell them we’re coming.”  

“You and your bloody biscuits,” she panted. “Just drive.”  

I was. I drove with white-knuckled terror, hurtling through the night, the headlights devouring the pulsating white lines like a racing heartbeat. It felt like an eternity before I veered off the freeway at Scottburgh. Barely slowing, first right, first left, tearing through gears, tyres squealing, I raced up the hill to the hospital at the top.  

Out of nowhere, as we crested the hill, the halogens lit up a red and white striped boom gate which hadn’t been there on our trial run last week. The boom lay rigidly across the road with a big red sign on it commanding us to “STOP.”  

“Aargh!” I slammed on the brakes and pounded the klaxon. Anne instinctively threw her hands forward and braced herself on the dashboard. The guard stumbled clumsily out of the kiosk and fell onto the counterweight just in time. The stop sign vanished and I heard it twang the tip of the aerial as we shot by underneath.  

From clear across the midnight car park, I aimed straight for the wheelchair ramp in front of the main doors. On the trial run, I had noticed how narrow it was: barely wider than the car. I’d also noticed that the huge double doors opened automatically if anyone was on the ramp. Eyes trained on the railings like gun sights, I lined the car up and flew straight at it. At the very last second I bailed, stomped on the brakes and squealed to a rather inelegant halt just short of the ramp. The large hospital doors slid open anyway. I sat there for a moment, considering, revving the engine. “Don’t you dare...” Anne said, opening her door. I shivered for a moment at the thought of what might’ve happened if I’d actually tried it.  

A night nurse shielded her eyes from the headlights and turned to say something to someone I couldn’t see. She stared back at us trying to see who it was. I switched off the lights and the engine and got out of the car.  

“At least someone’s awake,” I said to Anne with relief.  

“It’s. A. Hospital.”  
  

### The Philistine  

We hobbled as quickly as we could through the open doors into the foyer and with all the urgency at my disposal, I blurted out, “We’re having a baby...the contractions are less than two seconds apart and I think her pubix is fully diluted.”  

No answer.  

What I meant to say was, of course, that my wife was having the baby, the contractions were less than two minutes apart and that her cervix must be fully dilated. I thought I was being awfully helpful, actually, and I reasonably expected all manner of emergency-room activity to follow. You know, nurses and doctors flurrying urgently and purposefully, lights, equipment, life-support doctor-speak and the like; but there was nothing. The loudest noise in the stillness of the cold night air was the faint ticking of my engine as it cooled down outside and the monitored whooshing of the sliding doors which didn’t know whether to stay open or closed. Then, from behind a portable cane room divider, I heard what sounded like the easy creaking of a wicker-work chair as someone slowly got up. This is positively dismal, I thought.  

“Can I have some assistance please?”  

The nurse who had been hidden now came out slowly from behind the makeshift room divider which I surmised had been put there so she could sleep without being seen. She had a slight limp from sleeping in an awkward position, no doubt. I had begun to pace to-and-fro a little, anxiously looking from one nurse to the other, willing them to do or say something.  

“Mrs King?” the wicker-work nurse asked as she approached Anne. Anne, who was bent over as far as her swollen belly would allow, nodded and gasped, “Yes.”  

“Come this way, my dear,” she said, extending a warm, beckoning, motherly arm towards Anne, “We’ve got everything ready for you.” I moved forward with Anne but was stopped by that very same arm which had now become cold and rigid and exclusionary. “No, no, Mr King. Not you, yet. You stay here and fill out a few forms with nurse while I get Mrs King ready. Oh, and be a dear; move your car, won’t you? It’s keeping the doors open and letting all the warm air out,” she said as she excised me off Anne like a wart. I watched the couple hobble off down the corridor.  

“It’s my baby too, you know,” I said after them.  

After moving the car I went back to the nurse at reception. “I thought I was going to have to deliver the baby in the car!” I said proudly, trying to strike up conversation.  

“Fill...these...out,” she said sharply in time to thumping down three forms in front of me, unfazed by the recent melodrama. She hardly even looked up. The top of her head had a small bald patch. I leaned over the counter and saw that she had almost completed today’s crossword puzzle. Clever nurse, I thought. Sensing my relative proximity and inactivity, she slowly looked up and quizzically bayoneted me with her penetrating, slitty, green, cat-like eyes.  

“Yessss?” she said, hissing rudely.  

“Pennnn,” I said, resonating her impatience and reciprocating her impudence.  

“Here,” she retorted as she menacingly pointed her solid-silver ballpoint Parker pen at me. It was heavy and I weighed it appreciatively in my hand.  

“Nice,” I said.  

“Gift,” said the bald patch.  She sighed as she got up and, as if she’d rather be anywhere else but here, she walked off without a word.  

I quickly completed the three forms and had just started to look at the few remaining clues of her crossword puzzle when she came back. Her return brought curious whiffs of mothballs, mint gum, coffee and cigarette smoke.  

“Finished?” she asked as she reclaimed her pen from the countertop. I noticed she had claw-like fingernails.  

“Yes,” I said glancing down the passage.  

“Good.”  

Taking the completed forms, and her time, she deliberately checked every single one of my answers, her pen poised like a teacher’s, ready to strike out any error. Without flinching she crossed out one of my answers and wrote something in its place.  

“Problem?” I asked.  

“No,” said the head.  

I leaned over and saw that where I had written ‘HUMAN’ next to the question ‘Race:’ she had scribbled in ‘WHITE’. The Philistine. When she had finished checking all my answers she slowly looked up at me. Her eyes weren’t quite as slitty as I thought they had been, nor were they as green. Her face seemed rounder, too, and I swear there was also a glimmer of a smile.  

“Mr King, you can go to Maternity Ward 2 now, down the passage and then first left. Good luck,” she said warmly, trying to be nice. But before I could even thank her, she had terminated the conversation, shown me the bald patch again, and submerged herself into her crossword puzzle. So, I said nothing and set off briskly down the passage. I never saw her again.  
  

### How Bizarre  

Anne was already in bed wearing a thick, heavily starched negligee of well-used hospital green. I spent some time with her while she went through several more contractions. When she got to Level-D breathing we panted a silly song together, just as we had been taught in daddy classes, to control the breathing and the pain:  

“Ooh I need your love, babe, …Guess you know it’s true...”  

I even set up my own two-part panting harmony. The more we panted together the more I believed that either the baby was going to be born before the doctor arrived or I was going to hyperventilate myself into a flopsy stupor on the floor and miss the whole damned thing. I never really understood why I had to pant.  

The older midwife decided that Anne required a drip. So, I was ushered away to fetch a facecloth from Anne’s maternity bag in the ward. When I returned to the delivery room they still hadn’t managed to insert the needle and Anne’s arm was bleeding.  

“What’s all this?” I asked.  

The older nurse assured me that it was nothing, “Anne’s veins are a bit thin, that’s all. Jane’s having a go.”  

“Jane’s having ‘a go’?” I shouted.  

Her eyes glanced quickly away. I looked over Jane’s shoulder down at Anne’s arm and there, in the palm of her elbow, was a fat, pulsating, almost varicose, vein the size of an ugly earthworm. How could she not see it? The older nurse, to whom I had given the facecloth, asked me to go and dampen it. True, it was no good to Anne dry, so I rushed back to the ward, drenched the cloth (and most of the floor) in cold water and ran back to the delivery room. The needle was in, but there was blood everywhere and Anne was crying. I was livid. I was about to explode when Anne suddenly went into another Level-D contraction. Both of us were beginning to loathe that stupid Beatles song.  

“When’s the doctor coming?” I asked, impatiently.  

“Don’t worry, Mr King” said the older nurse, “Doctor is on his way, there’s still plenty of time before Doctor needs to be here. It’s your wife’s first labour isn’t it, and first labours usually take quite a long time, don’t they Jane, and we don’t want Doctor here too early, now do we?”  

I loathed the way she kept saying ‘doctor’ instead of ‘the doctor.’ It made me madder.  

Jane snapped on a surgical glove and carefully inserted her right hand up into Anne. After a moment she quietly reported, “Ten.”  

I looked at the older nurse. “Ten! The baby’s almost crowning and the bloody doctor’s not here yet? What’s going on?”  

Her face blanched.  

 “I’ll try phoning Doctor again,” she said as she scurried out of the room.  

There were fewer contractions now, and they didn’t seem to be as violent as before. I got that horrid, sickening feeling that something was wrong. Way wrong. When the older nurse came back, she was as white as a sheet. She looked directly and intently at Jane, like she was trying to send an urgent telepathic message to her but before I could launch a tirade of frustration and fear at both of them, a strange man walked in behind her and greeted us. Jane replied, “Good morning, Doctor.”  

This stranger was not our doctor; he was someone else’s doctor. I didn’t want this doctor. I wanted my doctor, my older and experienced doctor, not this...this intern, play-play, dress-up doctor. Heavens, he was way too young to be a doctor. He looked more like Tom Cruise in Top Gun. The effect that he had on the nurses was electric. They suddenly woke up, immediately dressed him in our doctor’s huge green surgical gown and tied him up. While he was snapping on his gloves, he looked at me, nodded, and then at Anne. He introduced himself to her and apologised for being late. His hair was still a little wet and I recognised the scent of expensive aftershave, but before I could object and ask where the hell our doctor was, he’d already begun rattling questions off at the nurses like he was firing at them. He assimilated their rapid and clipped answers; he studied the foetal heart monitor and he read Anne’s chart, everything almost all at once. Information went in and instructions went out. This boy, this doctor, whoever or whatever he was, knew what was what. He simply took control, levelled the wings, and flew. Fear and frustration left. I knew we were back on course. I don’t think he will ever know quite how much trust I put in him at that moment.  

Jane put a pill under Anne’s tongue. The doctor explained that the pill was quite harmless and served only to induce more contractions so that the baby could be born quickly. While we all waited for the contractions, he furtively took out a pair of what looked like pruning shears and made two of the most barbaric cuts I have ever witnessed. Nothing in antenatal classes prepares you for this sanguine horror. It still bewilders me that Anne felt nothing. Carmine blood flowed everywhere. It was a massacre. I nearly passed out. Then he calmly asked for the suction cup. The older nurse handed it to him. After inserting it carefully into Anne, he switched on the pump. It didn’t work. He stopped what he was doing and for a moment focussed his attention on the pump. His slippery, blood-soaked hands made swapping the hoses on the pump quite a clumsy, almost slapstick affair. The pump still didn’t work. He disconnected it and flung it angrily away from him and demanded loudly, “Wriggleys!”  

He carefully removed the suction cup from inside Anne and held out his hand, palm-upward and waited. Jane dived into the bottom of the cupboard, opened a packet, deftly assembled the two metal halves of what can best be described as heavy-duty braai tongs and smacked them down into his hand. In perfect timing with the rubber wet slap, all the lights went out and the delivery room and everything around us was instantly plunged into the pitch blackness of hell. No-one dared move.  

“We’ll wait a few moments for the generator,” the young doctor said. There was an element of ‘hurry up’ in his voice. I could feel the fear and frustration returning.  

“We don’t have a generator,” Jane said, her voice collapsing seismically.  

“What?” he screamed. “I NEED LIGHT!” he barked, “NOW!”  

Suddenly, Nurse Jane flicked and sparked her lighter.  

“Good,” said the doctor with a sigh of relief as he carefully and quickly inserted the huge tongs into Anne, trying to avoid making shadows while he worked. Jane did her best to hold the lighter in the right places without burning her thumb. They made an odd team, like really poor shadow puppeteers.  

I looked around the delivery room walls at the eerie, flickering shadows acting out their macabre scene. “How bizarre,” I said to myself, and I shook my head in disbelief. On the bedside table sat the foetal heart monitor which had been squishing and squelching away throughout the bloodletting and darkness. Next to it, was a table lamp. That’s odd, I thought. If the monitor is still working, then the lamp must also be working. Trying not to slip on the blood on the floor, I moved slowly across to the other side of the bed and switched on the lamp.  

“Better,” said the doctor, “Much better. Thank you,” he said without looking up.  

Moments later, out came a head, which turned, then came the shoulders, quickly followed by a long, slippery, bluish body and a long sticky black stool.  

“It’s a boy!” exclaimed Jane.  

“Hello Matthew, Gift from God,” I whispered, as tears filled my eyes.  
  

### Despite Everything  

Rarely in life are there times when the awe of the moment is so profound, so inspiring, that language simply cannot describe it. This was one of those moments. It was a glimpse of heaven, the gentle touch of Matthew’s newly appointed guardian angel, the presence of the Spirit; or all of those all rolled into one. You had to be there. You have to be a father watching his child being born. There is nothing in this world quite like it.  

The doctor carefully passed Matthew to Jane, who was still smiling. She siphoned off the mucous and wrapped him in a cloth. For some reason I was worried that she would drop him. I kept saying to myself, “Don’t drop him. Just don’t drop him.”  

“Matthew, meet your mother!” giggled Jane as she placed him on Anne’s tummy for a while.  

I don’t remember what time it was when I left the hospital. I don’t even remember if the hospital lights came back on or not. Time and motion seemed to have stopped for a while. As I stepped outside, though, I sensed that night was nearly over.  

“I am ‘Daddy,’” I said loudly and with dignity, as I patted the bonnet of my car.  

As I drove away from the hospital, I realised that I really didn’t want to go home to an empty flat, so I left the main road and drove slowly down the narrow promenade that runs parallel to the sea. I wound down the window fully and inhaled deeply the satisfying cool, salty sea air while the white noise of the breakers pounded handsomely in my ears. The promenade was deserted, as if it had been somehow specially cleared for my proud, one-man parade. I parked the car where the road is closest to the sea and decided to watch the sunrise. “New life, new day,” I figured, and lazily scanned the horizon for a glimmer of the sun’s first red.  

“Thank You, Lord...” I began, but that was as much as the stone in my throat was going to let me pray. Tears said the rest.  

Freely and humbly accepting the gentle nudges of the guiding hand of God furnishes life with meaning and clear perspective. I realised that Matthew was born despite my anxiety, despite my anger and frustration. Matthew was born despite everything that did or didn’t happen. Matthew was born because that’s what God wanted.  

I must have dozed off because by the time I woke up, the sun was well off the horizon. I was a bit disappointed that the day had dawned without me and although I knew I could catch the sun’s first red another time, it wouldn’t ever be the same. Never mind. A little further down the road was the Blue Marlin Hotel. I had been told that they served very good breakfasts there, and I was hungry.
